1. The Reality of Lean 4 for Undergrad MathWe started by looking at Lean 4 for Real Analysis and Abstract Algebra. While it is an incredible tool for forcing absolute rigor (especially with $\epsilon$-$\delta$ proofs and group theory structures), using it for weekly tutorial sheets is massive overkill.The Problem: The cognitive overhead of fighting compiler errors, type coercion, and syntax takes away from learning the actual math.The Compromise: Do your homework on paper first, and formalize just one interesting theorem per week in Lean to build your skills without burning out.2. Autoformalization (AI Translating to Lean)You asked if an AI could read your Markdown/LaTeX, translate it to Lean, and use the compiler errors to grade your logic.The Verdict: This is a cutting-edge research area, but currently highly frustrating for students. LLMs struggle to write perfectly compiling Lean code, meaning you wouldn't know if an error was due to a flaw in your math or a hallucinated syntax error by the AI.3. A Better Tool Stack for Pure MathInstead of fighting formal compilers, we discussed practical ways to study pure math:Flashcards (Anki): Only for exact definitions, theorem hypotheses, and canonical counterexamples (not full proofs).Obsidian: For linking atomic notes to build a web of theorems and dependencies.SageMath/Jupyter & Visualizers: Using computational tools to generate concrete examples of abstract concepts (e.g., viewing Cayley diagrams in Group Explorer, or searching topology counterexamples in $\pi$-Base).The "Drop a Hypothesis" Drill: A study technique where you systematically break a theorem by removing its conditions one by one to see why they are necessary.4. The Big Idea: Building the "Proof Intern"You proposed building a lightweight, highly readable proof checker that focuses on algebra and logic rather than deep formal axioms. This concept already exists in academia as Controlled Natural Language (CNL) proof assistants (like the Naproche project or Lurch), but there is plenty of room to build a modern, accessible version.   Here is the blueprint we mapped out for how you could actually build it:The Division of Labor: You don't build a theorem prover from scratch. You build the grammar, and farm out the math.Python (with lark) parses the English keywords.SymPy (CAS) checks algebraic steps (ensuring $\text{Step}_A - \text{Step}_B = 0$).Z3 (SMT Solver) handles domain restrictions (e.g., preventing division by zero) and basic inequalities.The Grammar (Scope Stack): Words map directly to code functions.Let x : Real declares a variable.Assume P pushes a hypothesis to a temporary stack.Therefore Q checks the goal and pops the stack.The Syntax: A hybrid Markdown approach. Indentation controls the scope (like Python), natural words manage the logic flow, and $ LaTeX $ handles the pure math expressions.The Interface: A live, dual-pane web app (built in Streamlit or similar) that evaluates your steps as you type (like a Language Server), instantly flagging algebraic slip-ups or missing domain checks with concrete counterexamples.
